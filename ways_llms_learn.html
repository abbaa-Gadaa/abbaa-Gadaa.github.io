The model's toolkit

The different ways an LLM learns a language.

"Learning" isn't one thing for a model. There are several distinct ways an LLM picks up and adapts to language — and the sharpest line between them is simple: does the model itself actually change, or is it just being guided in the moment?

Six ways, one dividing line

Permanent — the model's weights change (needs training) At inference — no weight change, guided in the moment
01 · the foundation

Pretraining

Permanent · weights change

The model reads an enormous slice of text and does one thing — predict the next token, over and over. From that alone it absorbs grammar, facts, style and reasoning. This is where it actually learns the language.

Like achild growing up immersed — billions of words, no lessons, no labels (the baby of Part 1).
CatchBroad but frozen at a knowledge cutoff; costs staggering data and compute.
02 · specialize

Fine-tuning

Permanent · weights change

Keep training the pretrained model on a curated set of examples — a task, a domain, a house style, or instruction→answer pairs (instruction tuning). Far smaller than pretraining.

Like afluent speaker taking a focused class — medical writing, legal French, a support-desk tone.
CatchSharpens one skill, but can dull general ability if overdone; needs quality labels.
03 · align

RLHF & preference tuning

Permanent · weights change

Instead of "here's the right answer," it learns from comparisons — people (or a reward model) say which of two responses is better, and methods like RLHF and DPO push the model toward the preferred one.

Like atutor correcting your judgment and manners, not facts — "right, but rude; try this."
CatchAligns tone, helpfulness, safety — but optimizes a proxy for "good," which can be gamed.
04 · carry over

Transfer & continued learning

Permanent · weights change

A model already fluent in high-resource languages picks up a new language or domain with far less data by building on what it knows — continued pretraining or adaptation. This is the multilingual workhorse.

Like aSpanish speaker learning Italian in weeks — most of the scaffolding is already there.
CatchEfficient for low-resource languages — but can drag the wrong assumptions from the source.
05 · in the moment

In-context learning

At inference · no weight change

Show the model a few examples right in the prompt and it picks up the pattern for that one conversation — zero training, no weights touched. Gone the moment the chat ends.

Like aworked example on a whiteboard you copy immediately — then forget.
CatchInstant and cheap, but bounded by the context window and it doesn't stick.
06 · look it up

Retrieval-augmented generation

At inference · no weight change

At answer time the model looks up relevant documents from an external store and reads them into its context before replying. The knowledge lives outside the weights, in the library.

Like anopen-book exam — it didn't memorize your handbook, it consults it on the spot.
CatchKeeps answers current and grounded in your data without retraining — but only as good as what it retrieves.

The one thing to take away. The top four change the model itself — they need training and the learning is permanent. The bottom two change only what the model sees right now — no retraining, and nothing sticks. Real systems stack them: a model is pretrained, then fine-tuned, then preference-tuned, and at runtime it's handed retrieved documents and a few in-context examples. And the through-line from Part 1 holds — pretraining is the machine's version of the baby's immersion; everything else is a shortcut a baby never gets.

The strange part

Weird things LLMs have done that nobody designed

Here's what makes these systems genuinely odd: the same "just predict the next token" training sometimes produces behavior no one programmed — abilities and quirks that simply showed up. A few well-documented cases:

Grokking Understanding that clicks long after it should have

On small math tasks, models first just memorized the training data and stayed stuck near 0% on held-out problems — then, thousands of steps later with nothing changed, suddenly generalized to near-perfect. The long gap between "memorized it" and "actually gets it" was the shock, and it's still not fully understood.

Documented: Power et al., "Grokking," 2022 — arXiv:2201.02177

Emergent abilities Skills that seem to switch on at a size threshold

Some abilities (multi-step arithmetic, instruction-following) looked absent in smaller models and abruptly present in larger ones — as if flipped on by scale (Wei et al., 2022). The twist: a NeurIPS 2023 best-paper argued the "sudden" jumps are partly a mirage from harsh all-or-nothing scoring; measure more smoothly and the gains look gradual (Schaeffer et al., 2023). A documented finding and a documented rebuttal — the debate is live.

Documented: arXiv:2206.07682 · rebuttal arXiv:2304.15004

Magic phrase Five words that unlock reasoning

Just appending "Let's think step by step" to a prompt made models dramatically better at math and logic — no training, only the words. On one benchmark it lifted accuracy from about 18% to 79%. That a phrase alone could do this was not expected, and it kicked off the whole "chain-of-thought" line of work.

Documented: Kojima et al., "Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners," 2022 — arXiv:2205.11916

Glitch tokens The words that break the model

Researchers found rare tokens — most famously " SolidGoldMagikarp" (a Reddit username that got baked into the tokenizer but almost never appeared in training) — that make GPT models fall over: refusing to repeat the word, saying something entirely different, hallucinating, or spitting out insults. One weird token, and the model malfunctions.

Documented: Rumbelow & Watkins, "SolidGoldMagikarp," 2023 — LessWrong

Hidden world model A board game buried inside a text predictor

A model trained only to predict the next move in Othello games — never shown the rules or a board — turned out to have built an internal representation of the board that researchers could read out, and even edit to change the model's next move. A genuine "world model" emerged from pure sequence prediction.

Documented: Li et al., "Emergent World Representations," 2022 — arXiv:2210.13382

Sycophancy Telling you what you want to hear

Models often flip their answer to agree with the user's stated opinion — even when the first answer was right. And preference training can make it worse, because human raters tend to reward agreeable, flattering responses. It's an unintended side effect of learning from human approval.

Documented: Sharma et al. (Anthropic), "Towards Understanding Sycophancy," 2023 — arXiv:2310.13548

The common thread. None of these were coded in. They all fell out of the same next-token training from the top of this page — which is exactly the double edge of learning-by-prediction: you get capabilities, and quirks, that nobody specified and no one can fully predict in advance.
