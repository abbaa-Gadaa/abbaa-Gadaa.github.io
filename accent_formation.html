How language takes root — and why an accent forms early and lasts.

A child becomes a native speaker in a specific order: listening first, then producing, with the sound system of the language locking in before the first birthday. That early commitment is what an accent really is.

Scope  prenatal → adulthood Threads  perception · production · accent · neural embedding Evidence  18 primary studies, cited

The one-paragraph version

Language acquisition is not a single event but a sequence of overlapping sensitive periods, each opening and closing on its own schedule. The sound system (phonology) comes first and closes earliest: infants begin as "universal listeners" who can hear the contrasts of every language, then commit to their native inventory between roughly 6 and 12 months15. Production follows the ear — cooing, babbling, then words — built on the categories the infant has already tuned into. Because the phonological map is set so early and then constrains everything layered on top, the age you acquire a language is the single best predictor of whether you will speak it with an accent1011. Underneath it all, experience physically shapes the brain — a process called neural commitment, visible even in the myelination of language pathways616. The rest of this page walks that sequence and the studies behind it.

Thread 1 · Perception

From universal listener to native specialist

The foundational finding of the field is that infants do not start with their native language — they start with all of them. In the first months of life, babies can discriminate the phonetic contrasts of every human language, including ones their own language does not use and that their parents can no longer hear5.

Werker & Tees's classic 1984 study made this concrete. English-learning infants aged 6–8 months could reliably tell apart a Hindi retroflex/dental contrast and a Salish (Nthlakampx) glottalized velar/uvular contrast — distinctions absent from English. By 10–12 months, the same infants had largely lost that ability, while infants growing up in Hindi- and Salish-speaking homes kept it1. The loss is driven by experience, not by maturation alone: the brain keeps what the ambient language uses and lets the rest fade. This is perceptual narrowing (or perceptual reorganization)17.

Kuhl's work added the mechanism. By 6 months, native-language vowel prototypes already act as perceptual magnets: a good exemplar of a native category "pulls in" nearby sounds, shrinking the perceived distance between them. Adults and 6-month-olds show this warping (their discrimination patterns correlate at r = 0.82); rhesus monkeys given the same stimuli do not — the effect is specific to human language learning2. Kuhl formalized this as the Native Language Magnet model3.

Perception doesn't just narrow — it splits in two directions at once. Tracking American and Japanese infants on the English /r/–/l/ contrast over the same 6–12 month window, Kuhl and colleagues found a double dissociation: American infants improved on their native contrast (63.7% → 73.8%) while Japanese infants declined on the same non-native contrast (64.7% → 59.9%)4. Native perception is being built and non-native perception dismantled in the very same weeks.

Why it matters for accents. By the first birthday the brain has committed to a set of sound categories. Any language learned afterward is filtered through those categories — which is exactly why the /r/–/l/ distinction that Japanese infants lose at 12 months is so hard for Japanese adults to produce in English. The accent starts in the ear.

Kuhl also showed that this learning is social, not just acoustic. Infants exposed to a foreign language through a live interacting person learned its contrasts; infants who got the identical material through television or audio only showed no learning7. And infant perception is predictive: better native discrimination at 6 months forecasts stronger language outcomes at 13, 16 and 24 months, whereas better non-native discrimination predicts weaker ones — narrowing on schedule is a good sign6.

Thread 2 · Production

How infants start generating language

Production lags perception — children understand far more than they can say — but it follows its own remarkably universal schedule. At the point a child produces about 10 words, they already comprehend roughly 60, and there is on average a five-month gap between understanding 50 words and producing 5013. The mouth is catching up to the ear.

The pre-speech ladder

Vocal production climbs a well-documented ladder, each rung driven by developing control of the larynx, jaw, lips and tongue:

Cooing / reduplicated vowels (~2–4 mo). Vowel-like "ooh" and "aah" sounds as the infant first gains voluntary control of phonation.

Marginal babbling & first consonants (~4–6 mo). Consonantal sounds appear, typically the front, visible p, b, m — the stuff of "mama" and "papa" across languages15.

Canonical babbling (~6–10 mo). The landmark stage: well-formed, repeated consonant–vowel syllables — "bababa," "dadada." Its absence by ~10 months is a recognized clinical red flag14.

Variegated babbling & jargon (~10–12 mo). Syllables vary ("badogu") and take on the intonation contour of the ambient language — it sounds like speech before it carries any words.

Babbling is about language, not just the mouth. Deaf infants exposed to a signed language "babble" with their hands — producing rhythmic, reduced sign-syllables on a timetable parallel to vocal babbling (Petitto & Marentette, 1991)19. That is strong evidence babbling is the language faculty coming online, not merely motor practice of the vocal tract. No other species does anything like it13.

From babble to grammar

Once syllables are in place, the lexical and grammatical stages arrive on a predictable clock1514: first words at ~10–15 months (often a label for a person or object); a ~50-word productive vocabulary and two-word combinations near 18–24 months; and grammatical morphology (plurals, regular past tense) emerging between ages 2 and 3.

Grammar development even leaves a fingerprint of rule-learning: the U-shaped over-regularization curve. A child first says an irregular form correctly ("went," "feet"), then regresses to "goed" and "foots" once they extract the "-ed / -s" rule and over-apply it, before finally sorting out the exceptions. Speech temporarily gets less adult-like — a signature that the child is generating rules, not just imitating13.

Thread 3 · Accent formation

Why age of acquisition writes the accent

An accent is, at root, a phonological system built for one language being used to speak another. Because the native sound map is committed so early, the decisive variable for pronunciation is when a language is learned — the age of acquisition (or age of arrival, for immigrants).

Across studies this is the most robust finding in the field: the earlier you start, the more native-like the pronunciation11. In a study of 240 native-Korean immigrants who arrived in the US between ages 1 and 23, the later the arrival, the stronger the English accent — and only 18 of the 240 scored within the native range on pronunciation12. Tellingly, age of arrival independently predicted pronunciation but not grammar (morphosyntax), which depended more on formal schooling — evidence that phonology has its own, earlier sensitive period12.

Is there a hard "critical period" cutoff?

This is where the semi-academic nuance matters. The popular idea of a sharp switch that flips at puberty is not what the phonology data show. Modern re-analyses find the decline in pronunciation with later age of acquisition is continuous and gradual — better modeled by a smooth curve than a broken line, with no clean breakpoint at 12–15 years1211. Some early starters keep a detectable accent; a few late starters get remarkably close to native11.

Two lines of evidence bracket the debate. On one side, even highly motivated, fully immersed adult learners (German TAs using German daily) did not reach native pronunciation — their performance did not overlap with native speakers'19. On the other, Bongaerts found a handful of exceptional Dutch learners of English whose accents were rated within the native range — proving native-like pronunciation after childhood is rare but not impossible20. The reconciling view: age sets the odds, but continued native-language use, quality of input, motivation and instruction (especially on prosody) all move the needle1119.

Phonology vs. grammar close on different schedules. Johnson & Newport's landmark 1989 study tied grammar attainment to age of arrival, declining linearly for childhood arrivals then flattening into large individual differences after puberty9. But the offset for grammar is surprisingly late: the largest study to date (669,498 people) puts the sharp decline in grammar-learning ability at about age 17.421. Accent is the earliest-closing door; syntax stays open into adolescence.

Thread 4 · Neural embedding

How language gets written into the brain

Why does early exposure matter so much? Because experience physically builds the machinery. Kuhl's phrase for this is neural commitment: as connections that respond to the regularities of the ambient language are strengthened during the first year, the brain becomes optimized for that language — and that same optimization then impedes learning of patterns that don't conform46. The native system becomes the default the brain runs on, and later languages must fight against it.

Statistical learning: the engine

Infants are powerful statistical learners. Rather than being taught words, they extract them from the speech stream by tracking transitional probabilities — how likely one syllable is to follow another. In continuous, unbroken speech, 7–8-month-olds use these distributional cues to find word boundaries (Saffran, Aslin & Newport's famous demonstration)57. The same distributional machinery tunes phoneme categories: infants pick up the frequency patterns of ambient speech sounds, and that reshapes perception7.

Learning begins before birth

The neural record now reaches into the womb. The auditory system is functional by about 30 weeks gestation, and the fetus hears the low-frequency envelope of the mother's speech through the final weeks of pregnancy8. Newborns tested within hours of birth already distinguish native from foreign vowels8. And EEG of newborns 1–5 days old shows enhanced, longer-lasting neural responses specifically to the language heard prenatally (French) — not to rhythmically similar or dissimilar unfamiliar languages — concentrated in the theta band that tracks syllable-rate speech22. The tuning has already started before the first breath.

You can see it in the wiring

Neural commitment is visible in brain structure, not just behavior. In a diffusion-imaging study, 30-month-olds who heard more adult words had higher myelin concentration in the core language tracts — the arcuate fasciculus and superior longitudinal fasciculus — direct evidence that language input shapes the physical insulation of language pathways16. The effect is developmentally timed (it runs the other way at 6 months), consistent with the idea that plasticity itself is on a schedule16. This echoes Lenneberg's old conjecture that language acquisition rides on brain maturation — cortical density and myelination reaching a threshold — which is one reason the windows close as the brain matures5.

The through-line. Statistical learning (the engine) + a maturing, myelinating brain (the timer) = experience-dependent neural commitment. Early input carves deep channels cheaply; the same input later has to re-carve against an already-committed system. That asymmetry is the biology behind "kids just pick up languages," behind perceptual narrowing, and behind the accent that outlives it.

Synthesis

What to hold onto

1. Perception leads production, and both are scheduled. The infant tunes the ear (universal listener → native specialist, 6–12 months) before and beneath the climb from cooing to babbling to words. Babbling is the language faculty switching on — deaf infants babble in sign on the same clock.

2. The accent is set in the sound system's early window. Age of acquisition is the strongest predictor of foreign accent because phonology commits first and hardest. The decline is a gradient, not a puberty cliff — odds worsen smoothly with age, with real exceptions at both ends.

3. Different layers close at different times. Phonology by ~12 months (with pronunciation advantages for starting before ~7–8); grammar stays learnable into late adolescence (~17). "Critical period" is really a stack of overlapping sensitive periods.

4. Experience builds the hardware. Statistical learning extracts structure from input; a maturing, myelinating brain turns that structure into committed neural architecture — a process that literally begins in the womb and is visible in white-matter myelin by toddlerhood.
