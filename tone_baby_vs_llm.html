Part 2 · a baby and a language model, side by side

Learning tone — the same trick, in two different worlds.

A baby and an LLM both discover "tone" without ever being taught it — by predicting their world and letting the structure fall out. But the tone each one learns is a different object. Here's where they rhyme, and where they don't.

Definitions first

What "tone" actually means

The word hides three different things. Keeping them apart is what makes the baby-vs-LLM comparison honest, because a baby and a text model are not always talking about the same "tone." Linguistically, tone proper means pitch patterns that make lexical or grammatical contrasts, and roughly half the world's languages use pitch this way1.

Sense 1 · Lexical tone

Pitch that changes meaning

In tone languages, the pitch contour on a syllable is part of the word. Mandarin mā / má / mǎ / mà = mother / hemp / horse / scold — same consonant and vowel, four meanings, distinguished only by pitch.

Sense 2 · Intonation / prosody

The melody of a phrase

Pitch riding across a whole utterance: a rising tail marks a question, a fall marks a statement, stress lands on what matters. Every language uses this, tonal or not.

Sense 3 · Affective tone

Tone of voice — emotion & stance

Warm, sharp, sarcastic, soothing. The paralinguistic layer that signals how the speaker feels and what they mean beyond the words.

Physically, all three ride mostly on one signal: fundamental frequency (F0) — how fast the vocal folds vibrate, which we hear as pitch (with help from duration and loudness). F0 is the earliest and most salient auditory dimension a human is exposed to, which is why it becomes the shared acoustic basis for tone, intonation and emotion1. A baby learning tone is learning to carve this continuous pitch stream into meaningful categories.

The baby

When — and how — a baby identifies tone

Tone perception rides the same developmental logic as the rest of the sound system from Part 1: start broad, then narrow to the native language. Pitch is one of the very first things an infant is sensitive to, because it survives the trip into the womb.

before birth
Prosody reaches the fetus — and emotion registers at birth. Low-frequency pitch and rhythm pass into the womb better than crisp consonants. Newborns' cries already carry the melodic contour of their native language, and neonates discriminate happy vs. neutral emotional prosody from about 37 weeks gestational age — activating the right superior temporal gyrus, modulated by the native language8. Affective tone of voice is one of the first things the brain reacts to.
0–4 mo
Pitch-tuned from the start. Infants discriminate pitch contours and are drawn to the exaggerated, sing-song pitch of infant-directed speech ("motherese"). Note the asymmetry: affective prosody is reliably read early only with a supporting face, and using F0 alone as an emotion cue isn't reliable until after age 41 — emotional tone is grounded in the whole social scene, not just the sound.
~6 mo
Universal tone listeners. At 4–6 months, infants from any language background — including non-tonal English, French, Dutch and German — can discriminate foreign lexical tone contrasts (e.g., Thai or Cantonese tones). The ability is not yet specialized3.
~9 mo
Tone narrowing. By 9 months, infants learning a non-tonal language lose the ability to discriminate those foreign tone contrasts, while infants learning a tone language keep and sharpen it — the perceptual narrowing seen for consonants and vowels, now on the pitch dimension (Mattock & Burnham; Mattock et al.)23. It arrives slightly later than vowel narrowing.
~12–18 mo
A twist: the rebound. For non-tone-language infants the loss isn't permanent — Dutch and German babies who fail at 9 months recover tone discrimination in the second year, making the trajectory U-shaped45. But this is regained general acoustic sensitivity, not lexical tone — they hear the pitch difference again without treating it as meaning.
1–2 yr
Tone becomes lexical. Toddlers in tone languages use pitch to tell words apart and lock in meanings — pitch is now part of the word. The specialization even reshapes the cortex: Mandarin speakers develop enhanced categorical tuning to native tones and longer pitch-integration windows than English speakers, atop a shared, language-independent relative-pitch code7.
The key move. No one hands the baby labeled examples of "tone 2 vs. tone 3." The categories are discovered from the distribution of pitch in ambient speech9, then locked in by a developmental window. Hold onto that — it's exactly where the LLM comparison gets interesting.

The language model

How an LLM identifies tone

A text LLM has no ears. It never hears F0, never hears a voice rise into a question. So when we say a model "picks up on tone," we mean something specific: it infers register, sentiment, stance, and emotional coloring from the words, syntax and punctuation alone — the shadow that spoken tone casts onto text ("I'm fine.", ALL CAPS, a trailing "…", a curt one-word reply).

It's a side effect of predicting the next token

An LLM is trained only to predict the next token over enormous amounts of text. But to do that well, it has to model tone: a formal email and an angry tweet continue very differently, so a model that ignored register would predict badly. Tone-sensitivity emerges because it lowers the loss — not because anyone labeled it. This is the same "discover structure from prediction" logic as the baby, just with a different objective.

Tone lives as a direction in representation space

Strikingly, tone often ends up encoded in a compact, almost linear way inside the network. In a famous 2017 result, an unsupervised 4,096-unit model trained only to predict the next character in 82 million Amazon reviews developed a single unit — the "sentiment neuron" — that tracked positive vs. negative sentiment with no sentiment labels ever provided; overwriting that one unit's value even steers the sentiment of what the model generates10. More broadly, the linear representation hypothesis holds that features like sentiment and formality correspond to directions in activation space that you can probe for and steer along11. Recent probing work finds a single sentiment direction that multiple methods agree on, and shows it is causally load-bearing — ablate it and sentiment-task accuracy collapses from 100% to 62%12.

One detail is almost poetic: that sentiment isn't stored only on obviously emotional words. Models summarize tone at neutral positions — punctuation and names — so that ablating the direction at the commas alone already drops accuracy sharply12. The model builds a running estimate of "how does this feel" and parks it on the scaffolding of the sentence.

Attention supplies the context tone needs. Tone is contextual — "great, another meeting" is only sarcastic given surrounding cues. The transformer's self-attention lets each token pull in that context, which is how a model resolves sarcasm, irony, and register from text.

Speech LLMs get closer to the baby's channel

Text models are the mismatched case. But speech and multimodal models that take audio can process F0 and prosody directly. Self-supervised speech units turn out to implicitly carry emotional information — one system reaches ~61% emotion recognition from units alone and preserves relative pitch contours through reconstruction15 — and models with explicit pitch tokens develop emergent emotion- and emphasis-recognition purely from pre-training15. Those systems learn tone from something much closer to what the baby hears, which narrows the signal gap (though not the grounding gap) considerably.

The answer

So how similar is it, really?

Same method,
different world
The algorithm rhymes closely. The thing being learned does not.

Where they genuinely rhyme: both are self-supervised, distributional learners. Neither is given labeled "tone." Both discover it as latent structure because it helps them predict — the baby anticipating a caregiver's world, the model minimizing next-token loss. The mechanism the baby uses, tracking transitional probabilities in the input, is domain-general enough to work on non-linguistic tone sequences too9, and it's the same "structure from prediction" logic that gives an LLM a sentiment direction. Both wind up representing tone as a reusable internal dimension, and both specialize to their input distribution: the baby narrows to its native tone system, the model's tone sense reflects (and is biased by) its training corpus.

Where the analogy breaks: the baby learns tone as physics grounded in feeling — real pitch from a real voice, tied to emotion, faces, and a caregiver who responds. The text LLM learns tone as a statistical shadow in symbols, with no felt affect behind it (though how absolute that "grounding" gap is remains actively debated16). Three hard asymmetries remain. Data: children reach comparable emergent abilities on a few million words, while LLMs need 3–4, up to 6, orders of magnitude more language data14. The window: the baby's specialization is enforced by a developmental critical period — and tellingly, standard LMs don't develop one from statistical learning alone; researchers had to bolt on an artificial maturational constraint (a regularizer) to reproduce critical-period behavior, and without it LMs just catastrophically forget instead13. That's strong evidence the baby's window is a biological add-on, not an automatic consequence of being a good learner. The loop: the baby also produces tone and hears itself, closing a perceive-act circuit a text model lacks. So: a similar learning trick applied to two very different objects.

Put simply: the baby and the model are both brilliant pattern-finders who were never taught tone and figured it out anyway. But the baby is learning what a voice is; the model is learning what people write as if a voice were there.
